# Garden + Pauwels — Femoral Neck Companion Classifications

**Canonical deep reference. Apply alongside AO/OTA 31-B.**

These two systems answer different questions about a femoral neck fracture:

| System | Question it answers | What it predicts |
|---|---|---|
| **Garden** | How displaced is it? | **AVN risk** — risk to the femoral head's blood supply |
| **Pauwels** | What angle is the fracture line? | **Mechanical risk** — failure of fixation under load |

Most clinical decisions for femoral neck fractures (fixation vs arthroplasty; which fixation) are informed by Garden, Pauwels, and the patient's age and functional baseline together.

---

## 1. Garden classification (4 grades)

Originally described by R.S. Garden in 1961 for subcapital femoral neck fractures. The original system was based on the trabecular pattern visible on AP radiographs — specifically, the relationship of the primary compressive trabeculae of the femoral head to those of the acetabulum.

### Garden Grade I — Incomplete, impacted (valgus)

The fracture line does not traverse the full width of the neck. The femoral head is **impacted in valgus** (tilted into a more vertical orientation relative to the neck). The medial cortex is broken; the lateral cortex is intact.

- **Radiographic clue:** the principal compressive trabeculae of the head are angled more vertically than normal — the head appears to be "tipped" upward.
- **Stability:** the most stable of the four. Often united in situ if managed appropriately.

### Garden Grade II — Complete, non-displaced

The fracture line traverses the full width of the neck, but there is **no significant displacement**. The trabecular pattern of the head and the neck align essentially as in a normal hip.

- **Radiographic clue:** complete line, but the head sits in its normal anatomical position on the neck.
- **Stability:** stable to slightly unstable; fixation has a reasonable record, but late displacement is described.

### Garden Grade III — Complete, partially displaced

The fracture line is complete, and the head fragment is **partially displaced relative to the neck**. Critically, **the head fragment maintains some alignment with the acetabulum** — the femoral head has been pulled into varus by the abductors, but the relationship with the acetabulum is not yet fully restored.

- **Radiographic clue on AP:** the trabecular pattern of the head no longer aligns with the trabecular pattern of the neck; instead, the head's trabeculae are angled more horizontally relative to the acetabulum.
- **The "double-density sign"** — the head and the acetabulum overlap on AP, creating a dense crescent of overlapping bone. Present in Grade III.

### Garden Grade IV — Complete, fully displaced

The fracture line is complete, the head fragment has rotated, and **the trabecular pattern of the head re-aligns with the acetabulum**. Paradoxically, on the AP view the head can appear "anatomic" again — but only because the head fragment has rotated independently of the neck.

- **Radiographic clue:** the trabecular pattern of the head appears parallel to the acetabular trabeculae (as in a normal hip), but the neck is clearly out of alignment with the head.
- **The "double-density sign" disappears** — because the head has rotated to align with the acetabulum, the overlap that produced the sign is gone.
- **Stability:** the most unstable. Highest AVN risk.

### Simplified clinical use: Garden I/II vs III/IV

In modern practice, the four grades are often collapsed to two categories that map to treatment:

- **Garden I/II** — non-displaced or impacted → **fixation** is reasonable; AVN risk is lower (~10–15%).
- **Garden III/IV** — displaced → **arthroplasty** is widely preferred in elderly patients because AVN risk is high (~25–35% or more for Grade IV) and fixation carries a meaningful re-operation rate.

This binary simplification is what many surgeons actually use day-to-day, but the four-grade system remains the standard for registries and publications.

## 2. Pauwels classification (3 types)

Friedrich Pauwels described this system in 1935, based on the **angle of the fracture line from the horizontal** on an AP radiograph. The biomechanical insight: the more vertical the fracture line, the greater the shear component of the load across it, and the greater the tendency for the construct (if fixation is used) to fail.

### Pauwels Type I — < 30° from horizontal

The fracture line is relatively horizontal. Loads across the fracture are predominantly **compressive**, which favours union and resists construct failure.

- **Clinical relevance:** the most stable Pauwels type. Fixation tends to do well from a purely mechanical standpoint.

### Pauwels Type II — 30° to 50° from horizontal

The fracture line is intermediate. Both compressive and shear components are significant.

- **Clinical relevance:** intermediate stability. Construct choice and reduction quality matter.

### Pauwels Type III — > 50° from horizontal

The fracture line is closer to vertical. Loads across the fracture are predominantly **shear**, which favours displacement under load and increases the failure rate of fixation constructs.

- **Clinical relevance:** the most mechanically demanding. Fixation strategies often emphasise constructs that resist shear (e.g. fixed-angle implants, anti-glide considerations). Some centres use a medial buttress plate alongside cannulated screws for Pauwels III in younger patients.

### Measuring the Pauwels angle

The classical method: a line through the fracture line on the AP view; the angle is measured between that line and a horizontal reference.

**Caveats:**
- The measurement depends on the projection. A rotated AP can artificially steepen or flatten the apparent angle.
- The measurement on a single AP can be misleading; some authors recommend measuring on a CT-derived oblique reconstruction aligned to the femoral neck.

## 3. How Garden and Pauwels combine in clinical reasoning

Neither system answers the whole question alone. They are read together with **age, functional baseline, and bone quality**:

| Pattern | Typical considerations (educational only) |
|---|---|
| **Garden I/II, Pauwels I**, young patient, good bone | Fixation with cannulated screws or DHS — discussed as a well-supported option. |
| **Garden III/IV, Pauwels II/III**, elderly patient | Arthroplasty (hemi- or total) — widely discussed as the predominant option. |
| **Garden III/IV, Pauwels III, young patient** | Difficult — the surgical decision involves balancing the high AVN risk of the displaced pattern against the desire to preserve the native hip in a young patient. Often urgent reduction + fixation with a robust construct is chosen, with full discussion of revision-to-arthroplasty risk. |
| **Garden I, Pauwels I** in any age | Often fixation, sometimes non-operative in selected non-displaced cases — but late displacement risk is real. |

These are **educational pattern descriptions**, not recommendations. Skill 07 (Treatment Mapping) surfaces the options; skill 08 (Implant Selection) describes the device classes. The treating surgeon makes the decision.

## 4. Common pitfalls

| Pitfall | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Garden IV mis-read as Garden II because the head "looks anatomic" on AP | Always look at the lateral view; check the relationship of the neck to the head, not just the head to the acetabulum |
| Pauwels angle measured on a rotated AP | Note rotation; consider re-imaging or CT-based measurement |
| Forgetting that Garden was described for **subcapital** fractures | Apply with caution to basicervical patterns — AO/OTA 31-B3 may be a better descriptor |
| Treating Pauwels III in a young patient as a "fix and move on" case | Surfaces the highest-stakes decision in this whole region — should always involve early senior input |

---

## 5. Inter-observer reliability

Both systems are imperfect:
- **Garden** has fair-to-moderate inter-observer agreement; the binary undisplaced/displaced version improves to substantial agreement.
- **Pauwels** suffers from projection-dependent measurement; modern CT-based measurement is more reproducible.

Skill 06 (Differential Reasoning) explicitly handles the case where Garden / Pauwels assessments span more than one category.
