# AO/OTA Segment 31 — Proximal Femur

**Canonical deep reference. Use this file as the template for expanding other regions.**

This file walks through the structured reasoning for the AO/OTA 2018 Compendium's segment 31 (proximal femur). It does **not** reproduce the Compendium verbatim — for authoritative text always consult the AO Foundation's official publication.

---

## 1. Scope of segment 31

Segment 31 covers all proximal femoral fractures from the femoral head down to the lesser trochanter region. Anatomically:

- **Femoral head** — the spherical portion articulating with the acetabulum
- **Femoral neck** — the cylindrical portion between head and trochanters
- **Trochanteric region** — greater and lesser trochanters and the bone between them, ending at a line just distal to the lesser trochanter

Anything distal to the lesser trochanter (subtrochanteric) belongs to **segment 32** (femoral shaft). Acetabular fractures belong to **segment 62**.

---

## 2. Type letters in segment 31

Unlike the typical A/B/C pattern for shaft fractures, segment 31 uses the type letter to indicate the **anatomical zone**:

| Type | Zone |
|---|---|
| **31-A** | Trochanteric region |
| **31-B** | Femoral neck |
| **31-C** | Femoral head |

This is the **first decision point** in classification: where, anatomically, is the fracture?

---

## 3. Decision tree

```
        ┌──── Femoral head involved? ─────► 31-C
        │
START ──┼──── Femoral neck (between head and intertrochanteric line)? ─────► 31-B
        │
        └──── Trochanteric (between intertrochanteric and subtrochanteric)? ──► 31-A
```

When the fracture **crosses zones** (e.g. extends from the neck into the trochanteric region, or from the trochanteric region distally toward the shaft), use the zone of the *primary* fracture pattern and document the extension separately.

---

## 4. Type 31-A — Trochanteric region

The trochanteric region runs from the intertrochanteric line proximally to a line just distal to the lesser trochanter. Type A fractures are extra-capsular (so the blood supply to the femoral head is not interrupted — different from neck fractures).

### Group A1 — Simple pertrochanteric (two-part)

The fracture line runs obliquely from the greater trochanter to the medial cortex above or through the lesser trochanter, producing essentially **two main fragments**. The medial cortex (the calcar) remains supported.

Subgroups (.1, .2, .3) describe the precise line orientation and whether the lesser trochanter is intact.

**Clinical relevance:** these are the "stable" intertrochanteric fractures — the calcar remains intact, the fracture resists collapse, and both sliding hip screw (DHS) and cephalomedullary nail constructs have a long track record.

### Group A2 — Multifragmentary pertrochanteric

The fracture has **three or more fragments**, typically with a separate posteromedial fragment that includes the lesser trochanter. The calcar is compromised — the fracture is biomechanically less stable than A1, with a tendency to collapse and shorten under load.

Subgroups distinguish the degree of comminution and the size of the posteromedial fragment.

**Clinical relevance:** loss of calcar support increases the importance of an implant that resists varus collapse. Cephalomedullary nails are widely used; DHS is still used but with more caution, especially with significant comminution.

### Group A3 — Intertrochanteric / reverse obliquity

The fracture line runs **transversely or in a reverse-oblique direction** — from the lateral cortex distally to the medial cortex proximally — across the intertrochanteric region. The biomechanics differ fundamentally: under load, the proximal fragment tends to displace medially and the distal fragment laterally, the opposite of A1/A2 behaviour.

**Clinical relevance:** the reverse obliquity pattern is the **classical contraindication to a sliding hip screw (DHS)**. The DHS allows the proximal fragment to slide laterally — but in A3 the proximal fragment wants to slide medially, so the DHS construct tends to fail. Cephalomedullary nails are typically preferred, with a long nail if there is any subtrochanteric extension.

> ⚠ **High-value distinction:** confusing A2 with A3 is one of the most common clinically meaningful errors. **A true lateral hip view is essential** to identify reverse obliquity reliably. An AP-only classification carries real risk of getting this wrong.

## 5. Type 31-B — Femoral neck

Neck fractures are typically **intracapsular**, which has two major consequences:
1. The fracture haematoma is contained within the joint capsule, raising intracapsular pressure and potentially compromising the femoral head's blood supply.
2. The retinacular vessels (the dominant blood supply to the head) run along the neck and are vulnerable.

This is why femoral neck fractures, especially displaced ones in elderly patients, are frequently treated with **arthroplasty** rather than fixation — the head is at risk of avascular necrosis (AVN) even if the fracture heals.

### Group B1 — Subcapital (just below the head)

The fracture line is immediately below the femoral head. AO/OTA further subdivides by displacement; the **Garden classification** (skill 05) is widely used as a complementary system for this group and groups B2/B3.

### Group B2 — Transcervical (mid-neck)

The line runs through the middle of the neck. Pauwels' angle becomes relevant here (skill 05): the steeper the line, the higher the shear forces, the higher the failure rate of fixation.

### Group B3 — Basicervical / displaced or shear pattern

Fractures at the base of the neck or with significant shear angles. Treatment is more controversial; some authors classify basicervical fractures as a transitional pattern between neck and intertrochanteric.

> ℹ **Key companion classifications for 31-B:**
> - **Garden** — 4-stage system, displacement-based. The strongest predictor of AVN.
> - **Pauwels** — 3-type system, angle of the fracture line. Predicts mechanical failure of fixation.
>
> See `skills/05-region-classifications/references/garden-pauwels.md`.

## 6. Type 31-C — Femoral head

Femoral head fractures are uncommon in the general population but classic in **young patients with high-energy posterior hip dislocations** (e.g. dashboard injury with a flexed knee).

### Group C1 — Split

Cleavage fracture of the femoral head, often after dislocation. The fragment may include weight-bearing dome.

### Group C2 — Split + depression

Both a cleavage component and an impacted/depressed area, often from impact against the acetabular rim during dislocation.

### Group C3 — Femoral head + femoral neck combination

Combined head-and-neck pattern — the most complex and prognostically the worst. AVN risk is high.

> ⚠ Always look for and document an associated posterior wall acetabular fracture (AO/OTA 62) and the direction of dislocation. The **Pipkin classification** is the commonly used companion system for femoral head fractures.

## 7. Worked example — applying segment 31 to a real case

**Case:** 78-year-old woman, mechanical fall at home. AP pelvis X-ray shows a fracture line through the proximal femur with a separate fragment at the lesser trochanter. No true lateral view yet.

**Reasoning:**

1. **Zone identification.** The fracture line crosses the trochanteric region. The femoral head and neck appear intact. → **Type A** (trochanteric).
2. **Number of fragments.** A separate lesser trochanter fragment is visible. This is more than a simple two-part pattern. → **A2** more likely than A1.
3. **Reverse obliquity?** Cannot be excluded without a true lateral. → **A3 remains a differential.**
4. **Subgroup.** Insufficient information without lateral and possibly CT. → Record as `31-A2.x`.

**Primary classification:** 31-A2 (confidence ~0.74)
**Differentials:** 31-A3 (~0.16), 31-A1 (~0.10)
**Required to refine:** true lateral hip view; CT if reverse obliquity is suspected on lateral.

**Why the differential matters:** if it's 31-A3 (reverse obliquity), DHS becomes a poor choice and a cephalomedullary nail is typically preferred. Misclassifying A3 as A2 and proceeding with a DHS is a classical and avoidable mode of failure.

## 8. What downstream skills do with a 31-x classification

| Classification | Downstream skill behaviour |
|---|---|
| 31-A1 | Skill 07 (treatment mapping) typically presents both DHS and CMN options as well-supported. Skill 08 (implant selection) describes the generic device classes and their considerations. |
| 31-A2 | Skill 07 leans toward CMN options but does not exclude DHS in carefully selected cases. Skill 08 highlights calcar support considerations. |
| 31-A3 | Skill 07 strongly highlights CMN, long-nail considerations, and the historical evidence against DHS. **DHS is not contraindicated absolutely** but the educational discussion of why it is usually avoided is surfaced. |
| 31-B1/2/3 | Skill 07 presents fixation vs arthroplasty options; skill 08 discusses cannulated screws, DHS-with-anti-rotation, hemiarthroplasty, and total hip arthroplasty, with the Garden/Pauwels companion classification as the dominant input. |
| 31-C | Skill 07 presents the open vs closed reduction question, the role of CT, and the complexity of head-preserving vs replacement options in older patients. |

In every case, skill 07 and skill 08 are **educational only**. They surface options and the considerations behind them — they do not recommend one over another. The surgeon decides.

## 9. Common misclassification patterns

| Mistake | Why it happens | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Classifying A3 as A2 from AP alone | The reverse obliquity is invisible on AP | Insist on a true lateral before finalising |
| Classifying a basicervical fracture (B3) as a trochanteric A1 | The fracture line position is close to the boundary | Look at whether the line is inside the capsule (B) or extra-capsular (A); use lateral; consider CT |
| Missing femoral head involvement in a high-energy young patient | Focus on the visible neck/trochanter pattern | In high-energy dislocations, always look for head fragments and check for posterior wall (62) |
| Classifying a pathological fracture as trauma | History overlooked | Always ask about pre-existing pain, primary malignancy, atypical features (transverse line on the lateral cortex in bisphosphonate users) |

## 10. Image-quality propagation

This reference assumes the case has come through skill 02 (Image Quality Check). If skill 02 flagged any of the following, **the 31 classification must restate them**:

- Missing true lateral hip view → A2 vs A3 differential cannot be resolved
- Missing pelvic AP → contralateral comparison and acetabular assessment limited
- Low contrast in femoral neck region → B1/B2 differential limited
- No calibration marker → templating for implant sizing not reliable

The downstream classification output must explicitly state which of these caveats applies.
